Why Do We Regulate Bank Capital? A 160-Year Story

Before diving into Basel III formulas, students must understand why these rules exist. Capital adequacy regulation is not an abstract exercise; it was forged in repeated crises where under-capitalised banks failed, depositors lost savings, and economies contracted. This tab traces that history from the 1860s through to the output floor debates of 2024.

Source materials: OCC "Moments in History: The History of Supervisory Expectations for Capital Adequacy Part II (1984–2021)" by Roger Tufts & Paul Moloney (Oct 2022), and OSFI "Basel III Capital Floor Technical Note" (Oct 2024).

Era 1: Before Basel — The Bellaire Decision & the 1983 Act

For the first 120 years of U.S. banking history, capital adequacy was assessed subjectively by examiners, combining capital ratios with judgments about asset quality, management effectiveness, and earnings prospects. There were no formula-based minimums.

The Bellaire Setback (1983): A U.S. Circuit Court ruled that the OCC had erroneously concluded that the capital of First National Bank of Bellaire created an unsafe condition. This decision was a significant setback to the authority of the OCC, FRB, and FDIC to make determinations regarding a bank's capital adequacy.

The law changed that same year. The International Lending Supervision Act of 1983 (Section 980) explicitly gave banking agencies the authority to set formula-based capital requirements:

"Each appropriate Federal banking agency shall cause banking institutions to achieve and maintain adequate capital by establishing minimum levels of capital… Failure of a banking institution to maintain capital at or above its minimum level as established… may be deemed… to constitute an unsafe and unsound practice."

Era 2: The First Rules & Basel I (1985–1992)

The OCC's 1985 Rule: Simple Leverage

The first enforceable capital regulation was a simple leverage ratio (capital divided by assets). It defined:

  • Primary capital: common stock, surplus, undivided profit, perpetual preferred stock, ALLL (minus intangibles)
  • Secondary capital: limited-life preferred stock, mandatory convertible debt (capped at 50% of primary)
  • Minimums: Primary 5.5%, Total 6% of adjusted total assets
Problem: A $100M Treasury bond and a $100M junk loan required the same capital. The leverage ratio was risk-blind.

Basel I: Risk-Based Capital (1988–1992)

The OCC, FRB, FDIC entered bilateral talks with the Bank of England and Bank of Japan. Their agreed risk-weighting structure became Basel I (1988), adopted by G-10 countries.

AssetRisk WeightCapital per $100

Key innovation: Off-balance sheet items (loan commitments, letters of credit, swaps, FX contracts) were included for the first time.

Minimum: Total capital-to-RWA ≥ 8% by Dec 31, 1992.

Bank Failures & the Case for Capital Rules

The data makes the case powerfully. From 1985 through 1993, 581 national banks failed. In 1988 alone, 123 national banks (2.8% of all 4,353) failed, the same year Basel I was announced.

S&L Crisis (1985–1993)

Weak leverage ratios, concentrated real estate lending, and lax supervision. The 1st percentile leverage ratio fell below 0% in the late 1980s, meaning 1 in 100 banks was technically insolvent.

Global Financial Crisis (2008–2010)

Over 500 banks failed. Basel II internal models had produced dangerously low capital. Banks continued paying dividends and bonuses as conditions deteriorated. This directly motivated the Capital Conservation Buffer.

Era 3: Basel II — The Rise of Internal Models (1999–2008)

The Basel Committee's 1999 consultative paper opened Pandora's box: letting banks use their own estimates to calculate capital.

Basel II Also Added

  • External credit ratings (S&P, Moody's, Fitch) for securitisation risk weights
  • Operational risk as a new capital charge (first time)
  • Three-pillar structure: Pillar 1 (minimums), Pillar 2 (supervisory review), Pillar 3 (disclosure)

The Transition Floor

U.S. agencies required a parallel run: Year 1 RWA ≥ 95% of standardised; Year 2 ≥ 90%; Year 3 ≥ 85%. This was prescient, but the 2008 crisis intervened before most banks completed the transition.

Key lesson: The financial crisis of 2008 fundamentally shattered regulators' confidence that banks' internal models could reliably determine capital requirements.

The Capital Floor: Why Models Need a Backstop

One of the most debated elements of Basel III finalisation is the output floor: a requirement that banks using internal models must hold capital at least equal to a percentage of what the standardised approach would produce.

Three Purposes of the Floor (OSFI)

⇅
Reduce Procyclicality

IRB models use historical losses: in good times, RWA falls, banks lend more; in downturns, RWA rises, banks contract lending. The floor dampens this cycle.

↔
Reduce RWA Variability

The Basel Committee found "significant RWA variability" across modelled banks for the same portfolios. The floor ensures a common minimum.

⚖
Promote Competition

Without a floor, large banks with approved models can hold systematically less capital than smaller banks using the standardised approach for the same risks.

COVID distortion (OSFI): "COVID-era fiscal support artificially lowered the number of defaults in historical data used for internal models, creating distortions that shouldn't reflect future downturns." The floor protects against this kind of model contamination.

The Floor Formula

RWA Floor Add-on = (72.5% × All-SA RWA - 12.5 × Total Allowances) - (Pre-floor RWA - 12.5 × Pre-floor Allowances) If the result is positive, the bank must add this amount to its RWA. If the result is negative (i.e., models already produce more RWA than 72.5% of SA), the floor is not binding.

Carter Golembe, arguably the foremost expert in banking regulatory policy history, observed:

"The never-ending search by regulators for their 'Holy Grail,' a simple formula or ratio that will encompass all that is needed to eliminate messy, hands-on supervision by tough, experienced examiners."

Basel II: The Three-Pillar Architecture (2004)

Basel II (published June 2004, implemented 2007–2008) was the most ambitious attempt to make capital requirements risk-sensitive. It introduced a three-pillar structure that remains the foundation of Basel III.

Sources: World Bank Policy Research WP 3387 (Powell, 2004), "Basel II and Developing Countries: Sailing through the Sea of Standards"; IMF WP/11/187 (Al-Darwish et al., 2011), "Possible Unintended Consequences of Basel III and Solvency II."

The Three Pillars

Pillar 1

Minimum Capital Requirements

The quantitative core. Multiple approaches to compute RWA for credit risk, market risk, and (new) operational risk. This is where most of Basel II's innovation (and controversy) lies.

Pillar 2

Supervisory Review Process

Supervisors assess whether banks hold adequate capital for their risk profile. Banks must have an Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP). Supervisors can impose capital add-ons.

Pillar 3

Market Discipline

Mandatory disclosure requirements so that market participants can assess capital adequacy. Includes risk exposures, risk management practices, and capital structure details.

Powell (2004): "In environments where banking supervision is weak, attempting to implement sophisticated ways of calculating required bank capital may simply give regulated institutions more ways to game their regulators." At the time, 50% of countries were compliant with only 10 of 30 Basel Core Principles. The average developing country: just 7 BCPs.

Pillar 1: Market Risk Under Basel II

The VaR Approach

Under Basel II, banks could compute market risk capital using Value at Risk (VaR) models:

  • 99% confidence interval, one-tailed
  • 10-day holding period
  • Minimum 1-year historical observation
  • Multiplication factor of 3× (or higher if backtesting fails)
Market Risk Capital = max( VaRt-1, mc × VaRavg,60 ) + Specific Risk Charge

The Fatal Flaw

VaR had critical weaknesses that the crisis exposed:

  • Not sub-additive: Combining portfolios could show lower VaR than individual books
  • Tail blindness: VaR tells you the threshold of the worst 1%, not what happens beyond it
  • Procyclical: Low volatility periods produced low VaR, encouraging leverage
  • Gaming: Banks could structure exposures to minimise VaR while retaining huge tail risk (e.g., super-senior CDO tranches)
IMF (2011): "Basel III strengthens the computations of market risk for extreme value events. Banks will now have to calculate a stressed VaR based on a 10-day, 99% confidence interval calibrated to a period of significant financial stress."

Pillar 1: Operational Risk — New in Basel II

Basel II was the first accord to require explicit capital for operational risk. Three approaches were available:

ApproachCalculationSophistication
Basic Indicator (BIA) 15% × average gross income (3 years) Simple — one-size-fits-all
Standardised (TSA) Business-line specific percentages (12–18%) × gross income per line Moderate — differentiates by business line
Advanced Measurement (AMA) Bank's own internal model (VaR-based, 99.9% over 1 year) Very high — supervisory approval required
Basel III change: All three approaches were replaced by a single Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) based on the Business Indicator Component. The AMA was eliminated after regulators concluded internal models for operational risk were unreliable and produced excessive RWA variability.

From Basel II to Basel III: What Broke and Why

The 2008 financial crisis was the definitive stress test for Basel II, and it failed. Over 500 U.S. banks collapsed. Banks that appeared well-capitalised under Basel II proved fatally under-capitalised when markets seized. This tab examines what went wrong and how Basel III addresses each failure, with particular focus on the trading book/banking book boundary and regulatory arbitrage.

The Trading Book Arbitrage: Why Boundary Rules Matter

One of the most damaging regulatory arbitrage mechanisms under Basel II involved the trading book/banking book boundary. Understanding this is critical for grasping why FRTB (RBC25) imposes such strict rules.

The Basel II Incentive Structure

Trading Book (Basel II)

LOWER capital charges

  • VaR-based: 99% confidence, 10-day horizon
  • For credit products: specific risk charge often minimal
  • Super-senior CDO tranches: near-zero VaR (appeared "safe")
  • No default risk charge separate from VaR
  • Diversification benefits fully recognised
  • Result: capital as low as 0.1–2% of notional
←

Banking Book (Basel II)

HIGHER capital charges

  • Standardised: fixed risk weights (20–1250%)
  • IRB: model-based but with higher calibration
  • Securitisation: look-through or ratings-based (high RW for sub-IG)
  • Full default risk captured
  • No diversification benefit in Pillar 1
  • Result: capital 8–100% of exposure

The Arbitrage Mechanism

  1. Originate a pool of loans (banking book: high capital requirement)
  2. Securitise them into tranches (CDOs, CLOs)
  3. Retain the super-senior tranche in the trading book (VaR near zero because "AAA" and low historical volatility)
  4. Capital dropped from ~8% to <1% on the same underlying risk
  5. In the crisis: super-senior tranches lost 30–60% of value, as VaR had completely missed jump-to-default and correlation risk
Al-Darwish et al. (IMF, 2011): "The tendency to move exposure off balance sheets because it reduced regulatory requirements ultimately led to utilisation of the originate-and-distribute business model, widespread use of conduits and overconcentration of exposures in less regulated areas of the financial system."

Unintended Consequences of Tighter Rules

The IMF (2011) warned that while Basel III addresses pre-crisis failures, it creates new dynamics:

Risks & Costs

  • Higher cost of capital: Deleveraging via debt-equity swap raises marginal cost. Banks benefit more from interest deductibility than insurers, so cost increase is asymmetric.
  • Risk migration to shadow banking: As regulated bank capital rises, risk moves to less-regulated entities (hedge funds, private credit, securitisation vehicles).
  • Interconnectedness via sovereign: Both Basel III and Solvency II increase demand for sovereign bonds (0% risk weight), strengthening bank-sovereign doom loop.
  • Reduced market-making: Higher trading book capital discourages inventory-holding, reducing market liquidity.
  • Funding pattern changes: Banks shift from senior unsecured to covered bonds, reducing unencumbered assets available to unsecured creditors.

Benefits & Safeguards

  • More resilient banks: Higher quality and quantity of loss-absorbing capital. CET1 focus ensures "real" equity.
  • Reduced TBTF subsidy: G-SIB surcharges, bail-in capital (TLAC/MREL), and resolution planning reduce implicit taxpayer guarantee.
  • Better risk measurement: ES captures tail risk that VaR missed. Stressed calibration prevents "fair-weather" models.
  • Reduced gaming: Output floor, strict boundary rules, desk-level approval, and RFET make it much harder to game capital calculations.
  • Macroprudential layer: Countercyclical buffer and D-SIB/G-SIB frameworks address systemic risk, not just individual bank solvency.
The fundamental tension: Capital regulation exists on a spectrum. Too little capital → bank failures and taxpayer bailouts (2008). Too much capital → expensive credit, risk migration to unregulated sectors, and reduced economic growth. Basel III attempts to find the right balance, but as Al-Darwish et al. note, "the joint implementation of the two accords will generate relatively stronger incentives for risk to migrate away from the two sectors" into shadow banking.

Why Capital Requirements?

Capital requirements ensure banks hold enough loss-absorbing capacity to protect depositors and maintain financial stability. The Basel framework, developed by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), sets the global standard.

Key Principle: Banks must hold capital proportional to the risks they take. More risk = more capital required. The ratio of capital to Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA) is the core metric.

What Counts as Capital?

  • CET1: Common shares, retained earnings, AOCI
  • AT1: Non-cumulative perpetual preferred shares, contingent convertibles (CoCos)
  • Tier 2: Subordinated debt (5+ year maturity), loan loss reserves

Risk-Weighted Assets: The Three Pillars of Risk

Total RWA = Credit Risk RWA + Market Risk RWA + Operational Risk RWA

Credit Risk (typically 80-85% of RWA)

Risk that borrowers default. Each asset is assigned a risk weight based on its credit quality. A government bond may be 0%, a mortgage 35%, a corporate loan 100%.

Market Risk (typically 5-10% of RWA)

Risk from changes in market prices (interest rates, FX, equities, commodities) affecting the trading book. Calculated via standardised or internal models.

Operational Risk (typically 10-15% of RWA)

Risk of loss from inadequate internal processes, people, systems, or external events. Under Basel III, calculated using the Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) based on a bank's Business Indicator Component (BIC) and historical losses.

Canada's Implementation (OSFI CAR 2026)

Canadian D-SIBs: RBC, TD, Scotiabank, BMO, CIBC, and National Bank. They face the Domestic Stability Buffer (0-4% additional CET1) on top of the standard D-SIB surcharge of 1%.
  • IRB Threshold: Banks with >CAD $5B regulatory capital or >10% international assets must use Internal Ratings-Based approaches
  • Capital Floor: Currently 67.5% of standardised approach RWA, which prevents internal models from producing excessively low capital
  • Buffer Breach: Only restricts distributions (dividends, bonuses), not operations

Trading Book vs Banking Book: The Fundamental Boundary

Every financial instrument a bank holds must be assigned to either the trading book or the banking book. This assignment determines which risk capital framework applies.

Trading Book

Market Risk Capital

  • Positions held for short-term resale
  • Profit from price movements
  • Market-making inventories
  • Hedges of trading book positions
  • Listed equities
  • FX and commodity derivatives
  • Positions in a trading desk
Key test: Intent to trade + ability to trade (liquid markets, daily mark-to-market)
↔

Banking Book

Credit Risk Capital

  • Positions held to maturity
  • Loans & advances to customers
  • Held-to-maturity securities
  • Equity stakes in subsidiaries
  • Real estate holdings
  • Retail deposits (liability side)
  • Positions not in a trading desk
Key test: Hold-to-collect intent, measured at amortised cost or FVOCI

Scope of the Trading Book (RBC25.1–25.13)

The Basel framework sets out precise rules for what must be in the trading book, what must be in the banking book, and what is presumed trading. These are not suggestions; they are binding classification rules.

What is an "instrument"? (RBC25.2)

Instruments comprise financial instruments, FX, and commodities. A financial instrument is any contract giving rise to both a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or equity instrument of another. This includes cash instruments and derivatives. Commodities include non-tangible goods such as electric power.

Preconditions (RBC25.3–25.4)

  • A bank may only include an instrument in the trading book when there is no legal impediment against selling or fully hedging it
  • All trading book instruments must be fair-valued daily with valuation changes recognised in P&L

Mandatory Trading Book (RBC25.5–25.6)

An instrument must be designated as trading book if held for any of:

RBC25.5 — Purpose-BasedRBC25.6 — Automatic Inclusion
  1. Short-term resale
  2. Profiting from short-term price movements
  3. Locking in arbitrage profits
  4. Hedging risks from instruments in (1)–(3)
  1. Instruments in the correlation trading portfolio
  2. Instruments creating a net short credit or equity position in the banking book
  3. Instruments from underwriting commitments (securities expected to be purchased on settlement)

Mandatory Banking Book (RBC25.8)

The following must be in the banking book regardless of intent:

#Instrument TypeRationale
1Unlisted equitiesIlliquid, cannot be fair-valued reliably daily
2Securitisation warehousingHeld for structuring, not trading
3Real estate holdings (direct + derivatives)Illiquid, long-term hold
4Retail and SME creditHold-to-collect business model
5Equity investments in funds (unless look-through or daily pricing available)Transparency requirement
6Hedge fundsLiquidity and transparency concerns
7Derivatives on any of the aboveFollows the underlying
8Hedges of the above positionsFollows the hedged position

Presumptive Trading Book (RBC25.9–25.10)

These are presumed to be trading book. Banks may deviate only with explicit supervisory approval and documented evidence:

#Presumed Trading Book
1Instruments held as accounting trading assets/liabilities
2Instruments from market-making activities
3Equity investments in funds (not excluded by RBC25.8(5))
4Listed equities
5Trading-related repo-style transactions
6Options & embedded derivatives issued from the banking book relating to credit or equity risk
Deviation process (RBC25.10): The bank must submit a request to its supervisor with evidence that the instrument is not held for any trading purpose. If approval is denied, the instrument stays in the trading book. All deviations must be documented on an ongoing basis.

Supervisory Powers & Switching Restrictions (RBC25.11–25.17)

Supervisory Override Powers

  • RBC25.11: Supervisor can require evidence that a trading book instrument is genuinely held for trading. If not convinced, can reassign to banking book (except RBC25.6 instruments)
  • RBC25.12: Supervisor can require evidence that a banking book instrument is not held for trading. If not convinced, can reassign to trading book (except RBC25.8 instruments)

Documentation (RBC25.13)

  • Clear policies and procedures for book assignment
  • Ongoing evaluation by internal control functions
  • Subject to annual internal audit
  • Results available for supervisory review

Strict Switching Limits (RBC25.14–25.16)

General rule: Switching is rare, requires extraordinary circumstances (e.g., permanent desk closure, accounting standards change). Market events or liquidity changes alone are NOT valid reasons.

Capital benefit prohibition (RBC25.15): If switching reduces total capital requirement, the difference is imposed as a Pillar 1 surcharge. This surcharge runs off as positions mature; it is never recalculated downward.

Approval process (RBC25.16):

  1. Senior management approval
  2. Thorough documentation
  3. Internal review for policy compliance
  4. Prior supervisory approval
  5. Public disclosure
  6. Reassignment is irrevocable

Internal Risk Transfers (RBC25.18–25.34)

An internal risk transfer (IRT) is an internal written record of a transfer of risk within or between books. The rules are asymmetric, designed to prevent capital arbitrage.

The Accounting Dimension: HTM, AFS & Trading Securities

The regulatory book assignment interacts with (but is not identical to) accounting classification under US GAAP / IFRS 9. Understanding this mapping is critical for regulatory reporting.

The SVB lesson (2023): Silicon Valley Bank held $91B in HTM securities at amortised cost, masking $15B+ in unrealised losses from rising rates. Because HTM losses don't flow through P&L or AOCI, the bank appeared well-capitalised until a forced sale triggered recognition. This highlights the regulatory tension between HTM accounting treatment and economic reality.

Boundary Rules Summary (FRTB — RBC25)

Rule Description
Presumptive Assignment Certain instruments are presumed to be in the trading book (e.g., instruments held for market-making, short positions, listed equities) or banking book (e.g., unlisted equity, real estate, retail/wholesale loans). The burden is on the bank to justify deviation.
Strict Switching Limits Moving instruments between books requires senior management and supervisory approval. Any capital benefit from switching must be forfeited (you can never reduce capital through reclassification). This prevents regulatory arbitrage.
Internal Risk Transfers (IRT) Banks may transfer risk between the trading and banking books via internal transactions. For credit/equity risk, only banking → trading transfers are recognised, and only through an approved internal risk transfer desk with exact external matching.
Trading Desk Structure Each trading desk must have: a clearly defined business strategy, a head trader, defined risk limits, and documented trading capacity. Desks are the unit of approval for IMA eligibility.
Documentation (RBC25.13/25.17) Banks must maintain written policies updated at least yearly, with criteria for book assignment, deviation procedures, extraordinary event definitions, and public disclosure requirements. Subject to annual internal audit.
Why does this matter for reporting? The book assignment drives which capital template a position flows into. Misclassification can lead to regulatory sanctions, capital add-ons, or trading restrictions. Under FRTB, supervisors can reassign instruments if they disagree with the bank's classification.

The Standardised Approach (SA) in Detail

SA Capital = SbM + DRC + RRAO

Sensitivities-based Method (SbM)

Decomposes each position into delta, vega, and curvature risk charges across 7 risk classes:

  1. GIRR — General Interest Rate Risk
  2. CSR (non-sec) — Credit Spread Risk
  3. CSR (sec, non-CTP) — Securitisation
  4. CSR (sec, CTP) — Correlation Trading
  5. Equity Risk
  6. Commodity Risk
  7. FX Risk

Default Risk Charge (DRC)

Captures jump-to-default risk not covered by credit spread sensitivities. Uses prescribed LGDs and risk weights by rating:

RatingRisk Weight
AAA0.5%
AA2%
A3%
BBB6%
BB15%
B30%
CCC50%
Unrated15%
Defaulted100%

Residual Risk Add-on (RRAO)

Flat charges for exotic risks: 1.0% of gross notional for instruments with exotic underlying, 0.1% for other residual risks (e.g., gap risk, correlation risk).

Internal Models Approach (IMA) — Key Requirements

Desk-Level Approval

  • Backtesting: Daily comparison of predicted VaR vs actual P&L. At 99% VaR, max 12 exceptions in 250 days (red zone = IMA revoked for desk)
  • P&L Attribution (PLA): Hypothetical P&L from risk model must track actual P&L. Spearman correlation ≥ 0.7 and KL divergence test must pass
  • RFET: Each risk factor must have ≥ 24 real price observations in past 12 months to be "modellable"; otherwise use stressed scenario

Capital Calculation

IMA Capital = max( ESt-1 , mc × ESavg ) + DRC + SES

Where:

  • ES = Expected Shortfall at 97.5% over varied liquidity horizons (10-120 days)
  • mc = multiplication factor (min 1.5, increases with backtesting failures)
  • SES = Stressed Expected Shortfall (non-modellable risk factors)
  • Floor: IMA capital ≥ 72.5% of SA capital (aggregate output floor)

When to Apply Each Approach — Decision Tree

Step 1: Is your trading book small?

Trading assets ≤ 5% of total AND ≤ threshold → Use SSA

No ↓

Step 2: Do you have supervisory approval for internal models?

No → Use SA (this is the default for all banks)

Yes ↓

Step 3: For each trading desk, do backtesting & PLA pass?

Yes → Use IMA for that desk (still compute SA as floor)

No → Use SA for that desk; re-test next quarter

Step 4: Aggregate: Total Market Risk Capital = max(IMA total, 72.5% × SA total)

Basel III Endgame (B3E) — What's Changing?

The B3E proposals, revised in March 2026, represent the final major overhaul of post-GFC capital regulation. They target large banks ($100B+ in assets) and fundamentally reshape how RWA is calculated.

Core Philosophy: Reduce reliance on bank internal models and increase standardisation, comparability, and transparency of capital calculations across jurisdictions.

Key Changes Under B3E

1. Expanded Risk-Based Approach (ERBA)

  • Replaces the Advanced Approaches for credit risk
  • Banks move from internal models to a standardised-plus framework
  • More granular risk weights for real estate, retail, and corporate exposures
  • New risk weight categories for project finance, specialised lending

2. Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA)

  • New standardised and basic approaches for CVA risk
  • Removes the option for internal models for CVA
  • Significant impact on derivative-heavy banks

3. Operational Risk Overhaul

  • Single Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA) replaces all prior methods
  • Business Indicator Component (BIC) based on revenue streams
  • Internal Loss Multiplier for banks with above-average historical losses
  • Expected 20-30% increase in OpRisk capital for large banks

4. Output Floor

  • 72.5% of standardised RWA as floor for IMA banks
  • Phased in: 50% (2025) → 55% → 60% → 65% → 70% → 72.5% (2030)
  • Eliminates extreme divergence between IMA and SA capital
