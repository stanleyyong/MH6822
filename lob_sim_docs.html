System Architecture

The simulation runs as a single-threaded event loop advancing one sim-second per tick. At each tick, agents are evaluated in a fixed sequence, orders are matched, fills are recorded, and the UI updates. The entire system is self-contained in a single HTML file with no external dependencies.

Limit Order Book Mechanics

Definition — Limit Order Book
A limit order book (LOB) is a continuously-updated record of all outstanding buy and sell orders at each price level. Orders are organised into two sides: the bid (buy orders, sorted descending) and the ask (sell orders, sorted ascending). The best bid and best ask define the inside market; their difference is the spread.
Figure 2 — Limit order book structure. Asks sorted ascending, bids descending. Best ask = 5892.25, best bid = 5892.00, spread = 0.25 (1 tick).

Price-Time Priority

Orders at the same price level are filled in the order they arrived (time priority). The matching engine in LOB-SIM implements strict price-time priority:

Matching Rule
while bestAsk() ≤ bestBid(): fill at price = asks[0].price qty = min(asks[0].qty, bids[0].qty) → crystallise fills, update agentPos, agentRealised → update lastPrice, totalVol, vwapSum

Agent Framework

Every agent in LOB-SIM has a type, a capital account, a decision function that runs each sim-step, and a state object containing all persistent parameters. The decision functions are called in a fixed sequence inside noiseAgentStep().

Principal vs Agency
A critical distinction governs P&L accounting. Principal agents trade for their own account — they bear inventory risk and their P&L reflects mark-to-market gains and losses. Agency agents (D1A, PBX) execute orders on behalf of clients; the client bears the economic risk, not the desk. Agency agents earn a commission minus exchange fees but never accumulate inventory.

Market Makers (MM1, MM2)

JPM-MM / GS-MM — Designated Market Makers
Avellaneda-Stoikov · Passive · Spread Capture

Market makers provide continuous two-sided liquidity by quoting both bid and ask prices simultaneously. Their sole objective is to earn the bid-ask spread. They carry no directional inventory — every fill is a spread capture event, not a position entry.

Decision Algorithm

  1. Remove all existing quotes from the book (removeAllByAgent)
  2. Compute adverse selection ratio: adverseFills / totalFills. If ratio > 60%, widen spread multiplier by 1.5×
  3. Scale quote size based on P&L trajectory and capital health. If P&L < −$2,000: small quotes. If capital < 50%: scale down proportionally
  4. Post 8 levels on each side at mid ± spreadMult × i × tick for i = 1..8
  5. Collect maker rebate ($0.25/contract) on all passive fills
Spread Multiplier Dynamics
spreadMult_new = spreadMult × 1.5 if adverseRatio > 0.60 spreadMult_new = spreadMult × 0.85 if adverseRatio < 0.30 and fills > 5 spreadMult ∈ [0.75, 4.0] quoteSize = f(P&L) × (availableCapital / startCapital) capped at capitalQuoteCap = ⌊capital / (11500 × 16)⌋
Adverse selection: an ask fill is adverse if price moved up after the fill (MM sold just before price rose). Widening prevents MMs from being repeatedly "picked off" by informed traders.
Reference
Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008). High-frequency trading in a limit order book. Quantitative Finance 8(3). Glosten & Milgrom (1985). Bid, Ask and Transaction Prices in a Specialist Market. Journal of Financial Economics 14(1).

Retail / Noise Traders (NT1–NT3)

Retail-A, Retail-B, Retail-C
Signal-Driven · Capital-Constrained · Rebirth on Bankruptcy

Retail traders have a weak directional signal (personal bias blended with recent price momentum), a capital account ($15k–$50k), and strict risk rules including stop-losses, breakeven trailing stops, and a "don't add to losers" filter.

Decision Algorithm

  1. Check checkMarginAndCapital() — if bankrupt, mark dead and schedule rebirth
  2. Update capital-derived position limit: posLimit = ⌊availableCapital / $11,500⌋
  3. Check breakeven trail: if unrealised profit ≥ 2 ticks, activate trail stop at entry price, move with market
  4. Check stop-loss: if ‖mid − entry‖ ≥ stopLoss ticks, flatten position
  5. If at position limit, or adding new margin would exceed available capital → skip
  6. Refresh directional signal every 30–90 steps: signal = 0.4 × bias + 0.6 × momentum
  7. If ‖signal‖ < 0.1, no conviction — skip. If holding losing position, 70% chance to skip
  8. Post 1 lot at best bid or ask (competitive price, not behind it)
Signal Computation
momentum = (price[t] − price[t−5]) / (tick × 6) signal = bias × 0.4 + momentum × 0.6 + noise ∈ [−0.3, 0.3] clamped to [−1.0, 1.0] entry if |signal| > 0.1 AND not adding to loser
Bias is initialised randomly as +0.4 (bull) or −0.4 (bear) at account creation. Bankrupted accounts receive a new random bias on rebirth.

Institutional Funds (FD1, FD2)

Vanguard / Blackrock — TWAP Mandate Executors
TWAP · VWAP Discipline · Front-Running Target

Funds execute a pre-determined directional mandate (buy or sell a fixed quantity) over the trading session using a TWAP (Time-Weighted Average Price) algorithm. They will not execute if the current market price is more than 3 ticks from session VWAP in the adverse direction. They pause when execution quality degrades.

Decision Algorithm

  1. Check capital floor: if equity < 30% of starting capital, suspend mandate
  2. Check execution quality pause: if average exec cost vs VWAP > 3 ticks over last 5 slices, pause 30–90 seconds
  3. Check VWAP discipline: priceOK = (isBuy → mid ≤ VWAP + 3 ticks) OR (isSell → mid ≥ VWAP − 3 ticks)
  4. If mandate complete (filled ≥ target), stop
  5. Place passive limit order of sliceQty lots at competitive best bid/ask
  6. Record execution cost for quality monitoring
Reference
Almgren & Chriss (2001). Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions. Journal of Risk 3(2).

Execution Desks (D1A, PBX) — Agency Model

Critical Accounting Distinction
D1A and PBX are agency execution desks. They do not trade for their own account. The client bears all inventory risk; the desk earns only the execution commission. Therefore: no inventory position accumulates in agentPos, and P&L = commission earned ($0.50/contract) minus exchange fee paid ($1.00/contract) = net −$0.50/contract. This is covered by the broader client relationship revenue.
GS Delta-One Desk (D1A)
Agency · Client Block Execution · 15% Participation Cap

The Delta-One desk executes index hedging mandates for institutional clients. A block arrives (representing a phone call from a portfolio manager wanting to hedge equity exposure) and is sliced over 30–90 minutes at a controlled participation rate.

Decision Algorithm

  1. With 0.033% probability per step (~1 call per 50 sim-minutes): generate a new client block of 500–2,000 lots
  2. Compute slice size: slice = 12% × estimatedMarketVolumeRate × 0.5
  3. Every 60–180 sim-seconds: place next slice as passive limit at best bid/ask
  4. Log completion with execution quality note when block is done
Slice Interval (adaptive)
mktVolRate = totalVol / (simTime_elapsed / 60) liquidAdj = min(1.5, mktVolRate / 40) [40 lots/min = baseline] sliceInterval = max(60, round(120 / liquidAdj)) nextSlice = now + uniform(0.7×interval, interval)
PB-Exec Iceberg (PBX)
Agency · Iceberg Algorithm · 12% Participation Rate

Simulates a prime brokerage execution algorithm concealing a large client mandate behind a small visible iceberg tip. The true order size (500–1,500 lots) is never shown to the market. The desk enforces a strict 12% participation rate and pauses if VWAP slips adversely.

Forensic significance of the iceberg pattern

A series of consistent small fills at the same price, regularly spaced, from the same agent ID, indicates a large hidden order. This is the canonical pattern surveillance systems use to detect institutional presence before large price moves — and what front-running agents exploit when they receive advance information.

Participation Rate Enforcement
targetFillRate = 0.12 × (totalVol / elapsed_minutes) actualFillRate = order.filled / elapsed_minutes if actualFillRate > 1.2 × targetFillRate: pause 15–40 seconds [filling too fast] if |mid − benchmarkVWAP| > 4 ticks: pause 10–30 seconds [market moved adversely]

SIG Arbitrageur (AR1)

SIG-Arb — VWAP Mean-Reversion
Statistical Arb · Confidence-Scaled · Hard Stops
  1. Check daily loss limit (10% of capital), cooldown timer
  2. If holding position: check stop (5 ticks) and profit target (8 ticks). Record win/loss, update confidence
  3. After 3 consecutive losses with confidence < 50%: pause 2–4 minutes
  4. Entry only if ‖mid − VWAP‖ ≥ 3 ticks and confidence ≥ 30%
  5. Size = baseQty × confidence (confidence-scaled position)
  6. Direction: short if mid above VWAP (rich); long if mid below (cheap)
Reference
Gatev, Goetzmann & Rouwenhorst (2006). Pairs Trading. Review of Financial Studies 19(3).

CTA Agents — Bet Sizing Strategies

The four CTA agents implement distinct position sizing methodologies from the systematic trading literature. All share: a 5-bar vs 30-bar price momentum entry signal (threshold 0.4 ticks), a 15% high-water mark drawdown shutdown, an institutional credit facility (borrow 50% of capital once), and distance-to-insolvency position scaling.

Shared Trend Signal
fast = mean(price[t−5..t]) slow = mean(price[t−30..t]) signal = (fast − slow) / tick entry if |signal| > 0.4 [any measurable trend direction]
Universal Risk Ceiling (all CTAs)
dist = availableCapital / startingCapital if dist ≥ RISK_SCALE_FLOOR (0.25): scaleFactor = 1.0 if dist < RISK_SCALE_FLOOR: scaleFactor = dist / 0.25 maxPos = ⌊posLimit × scaleFactor⌋ [Prevents any position when equity < 25% of capital]

CTA1 — Fixed Fractional (AHL-FF)

Position Sizing Formula
riskAmount = equity × riskPct [2% of current equity] stopLossDollars = 3 ticks × $12.50 [$37.50/contract] rawSize = ⌊riskAmount / stopLossDollars⌋ marginFloor = ⌊availableCapital / $11,500⌋ sizeQty = min(rawSize, marginFloor, maxPos − |currentPos|)
The triple cap (formula size, margin floor, risk ceiling) ensures the position never exceeds what available capital can support at CME margin rates, regardless of what the formula produces.
Reference
Vince, R. (1992). The Mathematics of Money Management. John Wiley & Sons.

CTA2 — Fixed Ratio (Jones-FR)

Unit Calculation
equityGain = max(0, currentEquity − startingCapital) units = max(1, min(8, 1 + ⌊equityGain / delta⌋)) [delta = $5,000] sizeQty = min(units × 2, maxPos − |currentPos|) On loss: units = max(1, units − 1) [Ryan Jones' own rule]
The Fixed Ratio method compounds position sizes faster than Fixed Fractional during winning periods but is more destructive in drawdowns — an explicit tradeoff documented by Ryan Jones.
Reference
Jones, R. (1999). The Trading Game. John Wiley & Sons.

CTA3 — Kelly Criterion (Winton-KC)

Fractional Kelly Sizing
W = wins / total_trades [from last 20 trades; default = 0.50] R = mean(rewardRatio of wins) [default = 1.50 if < 5 trades] fullKelly = W − (1 − W) / R fracKelly = 0.25 × fullKelly [25% fraction — standard practitioner] sizeQty = max(1, min(⌊fracKelly × maxPos⌋, maxPos − |pos|))
Quarter-Kelly is used instead of full Kelly to control variance. Full Kelly is theoretically optimal for long-run growth but produces ruinous drawdowns in practice. At W=0.55, R=1.5: fullKelly = 0.25, fracKelly = 0.0625 × maxPos.
Reference
Kelly, J.L. (1956). A New Interpretation of Information Rate. Bell System Technical Journal. Thorp, E. (1962). Beat the Dealer.

CTA4 — Anti-Martingale (MAN-AM)

Size Multiplier Logic
On WIN: consecutiveWins++ currentMult = min(maxMult, 1 << min(consecutiveWins, 3)) [1 win → 2×, 2 wins → 4×, 3+ wins → capped at 4×] On LOSS: currentMult = 1 consecutiveWins = 0 [full reset] sizeQty = min(baseSize × currentMult, maxPos − |pos|) [baseSize = 3]
The Anti-Martingale is the correct application of the "bet more when winning" principle. The Martingale (doubling on losses) is the famous gambler's fallacy. This strategy captures trend momentum: a 3-win streak produces a 12-contract position; the next loss immediately returns to 3 contracts.
Reference
Balsara, N. (1992). Money Management Strategies for Futures Traders. John Wiley & Sons. The article at utradealgos.com/blog/what-are-the-different-bet-sizing-strategies documents all four methods as used by institutional managers.

Bankruptcy and Rebirth

T1
Margin call check every step: if effectiveEquity < |pos| × $10,500, force-close position at mid, crystallise loss into realised P&L. Agent survives but position limit shrinks to match reduced capital.
T2
Bankruptcy check: if effectiveEquity ≤ 0, agent is marked dead. Position force-closed. rebirthAt = simTime + uniform(60, 180). Event logged.
T3
Rebirth (noise/rtm only): after timer expires, new account spawns: fresh randomised capital, new directional bias, generation suffix (Retail-A², Retail-A³). CTAs and institutional desks do not rebirth — they use a credit facility instead.
T4
CTA credit facility: when CTA equity first hits zero, one-time credit line of 50% of starting capital activates (agentCapital += creditLine). If equity hits zero again: permanent shutdown.

Market Abuse Typologies

Legal Basis
In the United States, market manipulation is prohibited under the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) §9(a)(2), as amended by Dodd-Frank (2010), enforced by the CFTC. In the European Union, Market Abuse Regulation (EU 596/2014, "MAR") Article 12 applies. Singapore is governed by the Securities and Futures Act (SFA) ss. 197–201, enforced by MAS. The IOSCO Objectives and Principles (Principle 36) require all member jurisdictions to prohibit market manipulation and insider trading.

LOB-SIM models six distinct manipulation typologies drawn from enforcement case law. Each is implemented as a separate agent strategy, activatable via the manipulation mode selector.

Spoofing

Spoofing — Fictitious Order Placement

Definition: Placing large visible orders with no intention of execution, for the purpose of creating a false impression of supply or demand, then cancelling the orders after the intended price effect is achieved.

Mechanism in LOB-SIM

1
Book imbalance analysis: MN1 measures depth on both sides. Spoof is placed on the heavier side (reinforcing existing depth for maximum visual impact).
2
Spoof placement: MN1 posts 200–400 lots 3–6 ticks deep. MN2 adds 60% of MN1 quantity at adjacent level. These orders are never intended to fill.
3
Real order: MN1 places 5–15 lots at best price on the opposite side, priced to fill immediately.
4
Cancel: Spoof orders are cancelled 2–6 sim-seconds after placement. They never fill.
5
P&L gate: Episode only fires if spread ≥ 1 tick AND cumulative P&L > −$2,000. Otherwise MN1 pauses for 5–10 min.
Expected Edge per Episode
realQty × TICK_VAL − realQty × FEE_TAKER = realQty × ($12.50 − $1.00) = realQty × $11.50 ≈ $57.50 – $172.50 per episode (5–15 lots)

Layering

Layering — Multi-Level Artificial Depth

Definition: Placing multiple orders at successive price levels on one side of the book to create an artificial impression of deep supply or demand, while executing a genuine order on the opposite side. Distinguished from spoofing by its multi-level architecture.

Place → Fill → Cancel Sequence

1
MN1 stacks 4–7 passive orders at consecutive price levels (e.g. bids at best−2, −3, −4, −5, −6 ticks). Each is 80–180 lots — an artificial "wall" of demand.
2
MN2 places genuine execution order on the opposite side (ask in this example), sized 30–60 lots. Other participants, seeing the apparent demand wall, move their prices.
3
MN2's ask fills at a price artificially moved by the apparent book pressure.
4
MN1 simultaneously cancels all layers immediately after MN2's fill. The cancellation of all levels in the same millisecond is the forensic signature.
Regulatory Treatment
EU MAR Article 12 is effects-based — demonstrating the market effect of the layered orders may be sufficient without proving intent. The US standard under Dodd-Frank requires proof of intent to cancel. This distinction is critical for cross-border enforcement.

FCA Final Notice: Michael Coscia (2013) — first European prosecution for layering. Fined £903,000 and banned. Documents the specific multi-level architecture.
Mark-Close

Marking the Close — Settlement Price Manipulation

Definition: Executing trades in the final minutes of the trading session with the intent of artificially influencing the settlement price or closing reference price, to benefit positions in related instruments (derivatives, ETFs, benchmarks).

Economic Incentive

Many derivatives, ETFs, index products, and portfolio performance benchmarks reference the official settlement price. A fund with a large offsetting derivative position can generate disproportionate gains on that position by artificially moving the settlement price a few ticks — even if it sustains a small trading loss on the manipulation itself.

Front-Running

Front-Running — Information Advantage Exploitation

Definition: Trading ahead of a known client order by exploiting advance knowledge of that order, to benefit from the price impact the client's order will cause. Constitutes a breach of fiduciary duty and, where the information is material and non-public, may also constitute insider trading.

Information Flow in LOB-SIM

1
Private information leak: MN1 receives advance knowledge of FD2 (Blackrock)'s imminent order — shown in the event log in pink as an INFO LEAK. This represents a private communication channel (phone call, electronic pre-trade disclosure) invisible to all other participants and to the tape.
2
Front-run position: 1 sim-second before FD2 executes, MN1 builds a position in the same direction as FD2's order.
3
Client order executes: FD2's large order moves the price in the anticipated direction.
4
MN1 closes: MN1 exits into FD2's price impact, capturing the move.

Why market data alone is insufficient for prosecution: The trade sequence (MN1 fills appearing before FD2 fills) is circumstantially suspicious but not conclusive. A skilled trader could independently develop the same view through legitimate research. Prosecution requires establishing the information channel: who knew, when, and how the information was communicated. This is why the front-running scenario places the INFO LEAK in the event log separately from the tape — it represents knowledge that no market data system can observe.

Key Cases
US v. Mark Johnson (2017) — HSBC Global Head of FX; first criminal conviction of a senior banker for front-running a client FX order. Key evidence: recorded phone call ("ohhhh f***ing Christmas").
FCA Final Notice: HSBC Bank Plc (2016) — £40M settlement for front-running Cairn Energy's $3.5bn FX order.
MiFID II Articles 27–28: Best execution obligations that front-running structurally violates.
Wash Trading

Wash Trading — Artificial Volume Inflation

Definition: Executing trades in which the same beneficial owner appears on both sides of the transaction, generating the appearance of market activity without any genuine transfer of risk or change in ownership.

Purpose and Economic Logic

Volume is a price signal: high volume is interpreted by algorithmic traders as confirmation of price direction and used in liquidity models and market impact estimates. Wash trading inflates reported volume with no genuine activity, creating a false impression of interest that may attract other participants to an otherwise illiquid instrument — historically used to inflate apparent interest in new listings, crypto tokens, or thinly traded products.

The Detection Limit

Invisible from Public Data
A wash trade is identical to a genuine trade on the public tape — same price, same time, same size. Detection requires identifying that buyer and seller share beneficial ownership. This requires exchange-level or regulatory-level cross-account data that is not available from any public market data feed. Gen-1 and Gen-2 surveillance systems cannot detect wash trading without privileged cross-account access. This is the key forensic lesson from the wash trading scenario.
Key Cases
CFTC v. Coinbase (2021) — documents wash trading in perpetual futures markets; highlights the beneficial ownership detection challenge.
SEC v. Lek Securities Corp (2017) — systematic wash trading in equities; volume inflation of 30–40% of reported volume documented.
Insider Trading

Insider Trading — Material Non-Public Information

Definition: Trading in a financial instrument on the basis of material, non-public information (MNPI) obtained through a position of trust or confidence, or through misappropriation.

MNPI in LOB-SIM

1
Private information event: After 30 sim-minutes, IN1 receives a private signal (e.g. forthcoming Fed Governor speech direction). This appears in the event log in pink — it is not visible to any other agent, not on the tape, and not in any market data feed.
2
Accumulation: IN1 quietly builds a position in the anticipated direction. Position size = 80% of capital / margin per contract. Adds 10–30 lots every 60 sim-seconds.
3
Public announcement: 10–20 sim-minutes later, news breaks publicly. All participants see it simultaneously. Price moves sharply.
4
Exit: IN1 closes entire position at the new price level, capturing the full announcement move.
Meulbroek (1992) Empirical Finding
On insider trading days, prices run up 40–50% of the total announcement effect BEFORE the public announcement. In LOB-SIM: pre-announcement price drift from IN1's accumulation is visible in the candlestick chart — a subtle directional move before the pink INFO LEAK event fires publicly.
This pre-announcement price run-up is the primary statistical signal used by surveillance systems to identify potential insider trading. However, it is circumstantial: a skilled fundamental analyst could independently develop the same view.

ETF Arbitrage Mechanics

SPY / ES Relationship
SPY (SPDR S&P 500 ETF) tracks the S&P 500 index at approximately 1/10th the index level (SPY ≈ $589 when S&P 500 ≈ 5890). ES futures have a $50/point multiplier. The theoretical "fair value" relationship: SPY × 10 ≈ ES mid price. Any persistent deviation between the two creates an arbitrage opportunity.

Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage Direction

Direction: Buy Low, Sell High
The correct arbitrage direction is always to buy the cheap instrument and sell the expensive one. When SPY is rich vs ES, sell SPY + buy ES futures. When ES is rich vs SPY, buy SPY + sell ES futures. The simulation models only the ES futures leg.

SPY Synthetic Price Model

SPY Price Evolution (per sim-step)
spyFair = midPrice / 10 [fair value, perfectly tracking ES] spyPremium × 0.9943 [mean-revert; half-life ≈ 120 steps] spyPremium += shock if rand() < 0.01 [ETF flow shock: ±2 ticks max] spyPremium = clamp(spyPremium, −3t, +3t) [arb bounds creation/redemption costs] spyPrice = round(spyFair + spyPremium/10) [actual SPY price displayed in top bar] spyPremiumTicks = spyPremium / tick [signal for ETA agent entry]
The ±3 tick bound reflects the real-world creation/redemption mechanism: authorised participants can create or redeem ETF shares at NAV (approximately), bounding the premium/discount. The 0.01 per step flow shock models independent ETF market events (dividend adjustments, large passive rebalancing flows that hit SPY directly).

Glossary of Technical Terms

Adverse Selection
The risk that a passive limit order is filled only when the informed trader on the other side knows the price will subsequently move against the passive trader. A market maker's ask is hit by an informed buyer who knows prices will rise — the MM sold just before a rally. Measured by the adverse selection ratio: proportion of fills where price moved against the passive side after execution. Used by: MM1, MM2 to dynamically widen spreads.
Agency Execution
A trading arrangement where the desk acts as an agent for a client, executing orders on the client's behalf. The client bears all economic risk (inventory, P&L); the desk earns only a commission. Contrast with principal trading where the desk takes risk onto its own book. Agents: D1A, PBX. P&L = commission − exchange fee only. No agentPos accumulation.
Avellaneda-Stoikov
A stochastic control framework for optimal market making. The key insight: the market maker's optimal quote spread is not fixed but adapts based on inventory risk (the more inventory the MM holds, the more it should skew quotes to reduce that inventory) and adverse selection (toxic flow widens spreads). In LOB-SIM this is simplified to: track adverse fill ratio and dynamically adjust spreadMult.
Best Bid / Best Ask
The highest resting buy order price (best bid) and the lowest resting sell order price (best ask). Together they define the inside market or touch. Any order that crosses the spread (buyer willing to pay ask, seller willing to accept bid) results in an immediate fill.
Cancel Ratio
The proportion of placed orders that are subsequently cancelled rather than filled. For directional traders, a normal cancel ratio is 20–40% (orders cancelled because the market moved away from the target price). A cancel ratio above 70% for large orders is the primary statistical signature of spoofing. Note: market maker cancel ratios are legitimately very high (80–99%) because they refresh quotes constantly — MMs are excluded from forensic cancel ratio accounting.
Confidence Score
An internal parameter (range 0.1–1.0) used by AR1, RTM1-3 agents to scale position sizes and gate new entries based on recent performance. Degrades on losses (× 0.80–0.85 per loss), recovers on wins (+0.10–0.12 per win). Below 0.3: agent sits out entirely. Below 0.5 after 3 consecutive losses: cooldown triggers.
Donchian Channel
A technical indicator showing the highest high and lowest low over the past N price bars. Used by RTM3 (BrkOut-C): when price breaks above the N-bar high, go long; when it breaks below the N-bar low, go short. The original "Turtle Traders" strategy. LOB-SIM uses an 8-bar lookback.
Effective Equity
The total economic value of an agent's account: starting capital + realised P&L + unrealised P&L. Used for margin call checks (effective equity < |position| × maintenance margin triggers force-close) and bankruptcy checks (effective equity ≤ 0).
Fixed Fractional
A position sizing method that risks a fixed percentage of current equity on each trade. Key property: position size grows with winning equity (compounding) and shrinks with losing equity (automatic de-leveraging). Used by CTA1. Risk% = 2%. Size = ⌊(equity × 2%) / (stop × tick_value)⌋.
Fixed Ratio
A position sizing method developed by Ryan Jones where position units increase by one for every $delta of equity growth. More aggressive compounding than Fixed Fractional in winning periods; more destructive in drawdowns. Used by CTA2. Delta = $5,000.
High-Water Mark
The peak equity ever achieved by an agent (equivalent to the NAV peak in a fund). Used for drawdown measurement: drawdown = (HWM − currentEquity) / HWM. CTA agents shut down if drawdown exceeds 15% from the HWM. This prevents deep loss accumulation and mimics real fund risk management protocols.
Iceberg Order
A large order that is deliberately broken into small visible tranches (the "tip") while the full order size remains hidden. Used by PBX to conceal the true mandate size. The visible slice (10–30 lots) is a fraction of the full order (500–1,500 lots). The regular, consistent small fills that result are the forensic signature of institutional iceberg execution.
Kelly Criterion
A formula for optimal bet sizing derived by John Kelly (1956): f* = W − (1−W)/R, where W = win probability, R = average reward:risk ratio. The Kelly fraction f* maximises long-run capital growth. In practice, "fractional Kelly" (25–50% of f*) is used to reduce variance. Used by CTA3.
Limit Order Book (LOB)
The complete record of all outstanding buy and sell limit orders at each price level, sorted by price-time priority. The central data structure of modern electronic markets. All orders in LOB-SIM rest in the LOB until matched, cancelled, or withdrawn by the agent.
Maker / Taker
A distinction in fee schedules. Makers post passive limit orders that rest in the book and provide liquidity — rewarded with a rebate (−$0.25/contract in LOB-SIM). Takers place aggressive orders that cross the spread and consume resting liquidity — charged a fee ($1.00/contract). Market makers are predominantly makers; retail traders and momentum agents are typically takers.
MNPI
Material Non-Public Information. Information that (1) has not been disclosed to the general public, and (2) if disclosed, would reasonably affect the price of the security or instrument. Trading on MNPI constitutes insider trading under most jurisdictions' securities laws. In LOB-SIM, the pink INFO LEAK entries in the event log represent MNPI events.
Participation Rate
The proportion of total market volume that an agent's fills represent, measured over a rolling period. Large institutional orders are typically executed with a participation rate constraint (e.g. 12–15%) to limit market impact. D1A and PBX both enforce participation rate caps. If filling faster than target: pause until rate normalises.
Price-Time Priority
The standard order matching rule: orders at a better price are filled first; among orders at the same price, the earlier-arriving order is filled first. LOB-SIM implements strict price-time priority in matchBook(). Aggressive orders hit the best price first; if that level is cleared, they "walk the book" to the next level.
Realised vs Unrealised P&L
Realised P&L is locked in — it comes from trades that have been completed (a position opened and then closed). Unrealised P&L is the mark-to-market value of an open position: position × (current mid − average entry) × $50. Total P&L = realised + unrealised. The agent card displays total P&L; realised P&L reduces the capital account.
Settlement Price
The official price used to value futures positions at the end of the trading day. For ES futures: the Special Opening Quotation (SOQ) of the S&P 500 index on expiry day, or the closing price on non-expiry days. The settlement price is the reference for: daily mark-to-market margining, expiry cash settlement, and many derivative and ETF NAV calculations — making it a target for marking-the-close manipulation.
Spread
The difference between best ask and best bid: spread = bestAsk − bestBid. In a one-tick market (the normal state in LOB-SIM): spread = $0.25. The spread represents the immediate cost of trading: a market buyer pays the ask and could immediately sell at the bid, losing the spread. Market makers earn the spread; aggressive traders pay it.
TWAP / VWAP
Time-Weighted Average Price (TWAP): the average price over a period, weighted equally by time. Execution benchmark for funds with large orders to execute by end of session. Volume-Weighted Average Price (VWAP): the average price weighted by volume traded at each price. More representative of the "true" market price during a session. Deviations of the closing price from intraday VWAP are a forensic signal for marking-the-close.
