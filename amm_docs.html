Technical Note Movmint Research April 2026

The Dark Forest, Indoors

A pedagogical Uniswap V3 simulator with concentrated liquidity, a public mempool, adversarial searchers, and the mitigations that try to keep them honest.

Pools 4 (5bp / 30bp / 1bp)
Agents 31 (LP, retail, fund, MEV)
MEV Modes 7 strategies + mitigations
Format Single-file HTML, no deps

This is a single-file simulator that puts a working Uniswap V3 in your browser, populates it with the agents that actually trade on it — passive LPs, retail traders, directional funds, statistical arbitrageurs, sandwich searchers, JIT bots, oracle manipulators — and lets you toggle the mitigations that have evolved to keep them in check.

The thesis is pedagogical, not predictive. Real on-chain MEV is observed; this simulator generates it. You can pause it, rewind it through the price chart, switch between modes to see how a sandwich attack reshapes a victim's slippage versus how a private RPC erases the attack entirely, and read the forensic accounting per block.

The model is a discrete-block continuous-time hybrid. Each second of sim-time corresponds to one second of (Ethereum mainnet 12 s, Unichain rollup 1 s) wallclock, and the canonical V3 swap math runs against actual concentrated-liquidity positions seeded into each pool's tick space. There are no decimals shortcuts, no constant-product approximations, and no pretending sandwiches are atomic at the protocol layer — they live and die by gas-tier ordering inside a builder loop.

The four modes of inquiry

This simulator is built around four questions that LPs and protocol designers have to answer in real markets, but rarely get to study under controlled conditions:

QuestionModeOutcome the sim measures
How does a public mempool let searchers extract from naive flow? sandwich Front/back-run profit, victim slip cost, success rate
Can a sophisticated LP crowd out passive LPs without taking IL risk? jit JIT mints/burns per block, fee share captured, passive-LP fee dilution
What does adverse selection (LVR) cost LPs over time? toxic Backrun count, single-sided LP IL accumulation
Can a price oracle reading this AMM be moved against an external protocol? oracle Push magnitude, hold duration, TWAP exposure window

Two more modes — protected and rollup — model the mitigations: private orderflow (Flashbots Protect / MEV Blocker) and short-block-time rollups (Unichain rollup-boost). Switching between them is the most instructive thing you can do with the sim. A run with sandwich attacks active and another run identical except for protected RPC will show the searcher's PnL drop to zero while the victim's slippage vanishes.

An AMM is a market structure. A sandwich is what happens when its rules of ordering meet a public queue. The simulator is the place to test what changes when either side of that meeting changes.

Open the simulator in any modern browser. There is no build step, no install, no server. Everything that follows is keyboard- and click-driven.

Five minutes to first MEV

  1. Open amm.html in a browser

    The page loads with all four pools seeded with realistic liquidity, agents created but idle, and the sim paused. The top bar shows the active pool's price, TVL, cumulative volume, and a UTC clock that drives diurnal flow intensity. The default time is 13:00 UTC — peak window for US/EU overlap.

  2. Hit ▶ START at the top

    Retail and fund txs begin flowing into the public mempool. You'll see them appear in the Mempool panel mid-page, ordered by gas tip. Every 12 seconds a block closes, the builder applies all pending txs in gas-priority order, and the price chart updates. The block-progress bar at the top fills toward the next block.

  3. Click 🥪 Sandwich in the strategy bar

    Two sandwich-searcher agents (SAND1, SAND2) activate. They scan the public mempool every sim-tick for victims with sufficient size and slippage tolerance, and submit paired front/back-run txs at gas tiers calibrated to wrap the victim. Watch the Forensics tab on the right — the Attempts and Profitable counters update in real time.

  4. Switch to 🛡 MEV-Protected RPC

    The forensics counters reset. Watch the Protected Txs count climb while MEV Extracted stays at zero — sandwich searchers can't see private orderflow. This is the single most direct demonstration of why Flashbots Protect and MEV Blocker exist.

  5. Use +1h / +6h / +24h to fast-forward

    The skip controls advance simulation state without rendering each step. A +24h skip will run through both peak and trough flow windows, letting you see how much MEV actually extracts over a real day, how much adverse-selection LPs accumulate overnight, and how stat-arb profits converge. The clock shows the current UTC hour and the flow multiplier.

  6. Click any agent card to see its decision rules

    The right-hand Agents panel lists every active agent with live capital and P&L. Clicking opens a modal with the agent's overview, decision rules table, and the academic reference for the strategy it implements.

  7. Download the swap log or event log

    ⬇ SWAPS exports every executed trade with block, sim_time, pool, side, size, price, agent, and tag. ⬇ MEV LOG exports the event stream — sandwich landings, JIT mints, oracle pushes, arb fires, block closes. Both are CSV for direct ingestion into pandas or Excel.

Things to look for

Sandwich profitability is fee-tier dependent

Watch the success rate carefully. On the 5 bp USDC/WETH pool a sandwich needs only 12 bps of victim impact to clear the round-trip fee. On the 30 bp WBTC/USDC pool the same searcher needs 65 bps. Real mainnet sandwiching is concentrated almost entirely on 5 bp pools for exactly this reason.

JIT P&L can go negative on quiet flow

The JIT searcher mints a ±10 bp position around current price. If the victim swap doesn't move price enough to lock the JIT into the in-range fee zone, the JIT can net a small loss after gas. The forensics panel shows Avg Fee per JIT — when this drops below ~$30 the strategy is barely breakeven.

Cross-pool dislocation is always running

The stat-arb agent watches WBTC/USDC direct versus WBTC/WETH × WETH/USDC synthetic and trades when divergence exceeds 25 bps. Even with no MEV mode active, you'll see the dislocation oscillate as fund flows hit different pools at different rates and the arb closes the gap.

Oracle attacks are episodic, not constant

Oracle mode fires a single large directional swap that holds for 8 blocks before reversing. You may need to skip a few hours to see one trigger. Watch the price chart — it'll spike, plateau, then snap back. The sim doesn't simulate the downstream lending-protocol exploit; the AMM-side is round-trip-loss for the attacker.

Note

All trade flow in the simulator is generated by the agents — there is no replay of historical mainnet data. The point is to build intuition for the dynamics, not to backtest a specific period. If you want historical replay, the CSV exports are schema-compatible with pandas DataFrames and a small loader script can swap them against on-chain events.

The simulator is one HTML file with one inline script. There are no external dependencies. Everything from V3 swap math to the chart renderer is implemented from primitives. This section walks through the four mechanical layers.

I. Concentrated liquidity math

Each pool maintains sqrtP (the square-root of price, in token1/token0 convention) and a list of liquidity positions, each with bounds (sqrtPa, sqrtPb) and an L value. The pool's active liquidity at any point is the sum of L over positions whose range contains the current sqrtP.

Swap step — selling token0 (sell0)
sqrtP_new = (L · sqrtP) / (L + Δx · sqrtP)
Δy = L · (sqrtP − sqrtP_new)
Swap step — selling token1 (sell1)
sqrtP_new = sqrtP + Δy / L
Δx = L · (1/sqrtP − 1/sqrtP_new)

When a swap would push price across a tick boundary, the simulator caps the step at the boundary, applies it, updates active L, and recurses — up to 32 hops per swap. This is the same algorithm Uniswap V3 itself uses, just in floating-point instead of Q64.96 fixed-point.

Why a display price exists separately

A subtle issue: V3's internal convention is price = token1/token0. But for USDC/WETH we want to display 3,400 (USDC per WETH), which is token0/token1 — the inverse. The simulator stores the V3-canonical internal price in pool.sqrtP and exposes a displayPrice(pool) helper that flips for inverted pools. All swap math, fee accounting, and tick arithmetic uses internal price; only the UI and USD-conversion layer touches the display side. This was the source of the worst bug in early development — see the Sandwich Forensics tab.

II. Mempool and block closure

Time advances one sim-second at a time. Every tick, agents may submit transactions to mempool and searchers run their detection routines. Once simTime − lastBlockTime ≥ BLOCK_TIME (12 s on mainnet, 1 s on Unichain), closeBlock() fires.

FIG. 02Block-closure ordering. Sandwich bundles are kept atomic; standalone txs interleave by gas.

The bundle-keep-contiguous rule is what makes sandwich attacks work. A sandwich front pays gas just above the victim's tip, the back just below — a naive sort by gas would scatter standalone txs between them. The simulator instead identifies bundles by their paired-id linkage and emits each bundle as a contiguous triple in the ordered queue. This is the same role Flashbots' block-builder plays on mainnet.

III. Searchers, agents, and timing

Each sim-second the loop runs searcherStep(), retailStep(), and fundStep(). Searchers see only public mempool entries — anything marked tx.private = true (set when MEV-protected mode is active) is invisible to them, simulating private routing.

Agent firing rates are modulated by a diurnal flow multiplier derived from empirical hourly DEX-volume distributions. Retail trade intervals scale inversely with the multiplier; fund firing rate scales directly. The schedule is bimodal — peaks at 13:00–17:00 UTC (US/EU overlap) and 01:00–05:00 UTC (Asia morning), trough at 06:00–10:00 UTC.

IV. Forensics and accounting

Every block, the simulator updates a forensics object with totals (swaps, volume USD, fees USD), MEV-specific counters (sandwich attempts/successful, JIT mints/fees, toxic backruns, oracle pushes), and the cross-pool dislocation in bps. The Forensics panel renders a mode-specific subset.

Per-agent capital and P&L are tracked in agentCapital and agentPnl dictionaries. For LP positions the P&L includes both fee accumulation and an impermanent-loss approximation (current USD value of the position vs original deposit USD). For active swap-based agents, P&L is updated in executeTx at swap time using the realised fill price vs the fair mid-block price.

Design choice — fees are credited per-swap, not per-block

A real V3 pool credits fees to active LPs proportional to L during the swap. The simulator approximates this by tracking fee0Total and fee1Total at the pool level and assigning each LP position its share based on its L over total active L at swap time. Per-position fee accumulators are updated in applySwap. This is correct for non-overlapping ranges and accurate within ~1% for typical overlapping configurations — the simplification is that we don't track a fee-growth-inside-tick variable per position.

Eight agent types populate the simulator. Each has a decision rule, a capital allocation, and a literature reference. The MEV searchers — sandwich, JIT, toxic, oracle — are off by default and activate only when the matching mode is selected.

The sandwich agent took four substantive iterations to behave correctly. Each fix exposed a deeper assumption that turned out to be wrong. This section documents the bugs and the choices that resolved them, in the order they were diagnosed.

The presenting symptom

Initial state: with sandwich mode active, the forensics panel showed 35 attempts → 0 profitable, $0 P&L. The searchers were firing, the events were logging, but no profit was ever recorded. Every attempt either vanished or counted as a loss.

Bug 1 — Bundle resolution failure

Symptom

The sandwich front and back txs were submitted, the victim was tagged sandwichedBy, but the profit-computation loop in closeBlock() never fired. The guard if (!(b.front && b.victim && b.back)) continue was always tripping because b.victim was null.

Bundle assembly worked by iterating over pending mempool txs and matching each to a bundle by pairedVictimId. The bug: the victim itself doesn't carry pairedVictimId. Only the front and back do. The victim is what the paired-id points to. So the matcher iterated past every victim and bundles stayed half-built.

Before — broken
pending.forEach(t => {
  if (t.pairedVictimId !== undefined) { // ⚠ skips victim
    for (const [fid, b] of bundleByFrontId) {
      if (b.front.pairedVictimId === t.id) b.victim = t;
      if (t.type === 'sandBack' && t.pairedFrontId === fid) b.back = t;
    }
  }
});
After — fixed
pending.forEach(t => {
  for (const [fid, b] of bundleByFrontId) { // ✓ scan all
    if (b.front.pairedVictimId === t.id) b.victim = t;
    if (t.type === 'sandBack' && t.pairedFrontId === fid) b.back = t;
  }
});

After this fix, attempts started resolving. But every single one was a loss — sandwiches were systematically losing $500–$1,800 each. The search continued.

Bug 2 — Inverted price convention

Symptom

Every sandwich attempt on USDC/WETH or WBTC/WETH lost money in a specific pattern: the front-run output and the back-run input had wildly inconsistent USD values, as if the swap math was operating on the wrong side of the price.

Uniswap V3's internal convention is price = sqrtP² = token1 / token0. Selling token0 pushes sqrtP down; selling token1 pushes it up. The simulator's swap math used this convention correctly.

But the POOLS config stored initialPrice values in the human-readable convention — "USDC per WETH = 3,400" rather than "WETH per USDC = 1/3,400". For two of the four pools (USDC/WETH and WBTC/WETH) the values were effectively inverted. The swap math was internally consistent but operating in the wrong direction for those pools — selling token0 (USDC) pushed the displayed price down when economically it should have pushed it up (more USDC chasing fewer WETH).

For the sandwich agent specifically, this meant the front-run pushed price the opposite direction of intent. The victim then pushed it further opposite. The back-run sold into a pool whose price had moved against the searcher every step — guaranteed loss, every time.

The fix — uniform internal convention with a display layer

Restructured all four pools to store internal V3 prices uniformly, with a separate displayPrice(pool) helper that handles the inversion for UI and USD conversion:

POOLS config — uniform convention
USDC_WETH: { token0: 'USDC', token1: 'WETH', initialPrice: 1 / REF.WETH, invertDisplay: true }
WBTC_WETH: { token0: 'WETH', token1: 'WBTC', initialPrice: REF.WETH / REF.WBTC, invertDisplay: true }
WBTC_USDC: { token0: 'USDC', token1: 'WBTC', initialPrice: 1 / REF.WBTC, invertDisplay: true }
USDC_USDT: { token0: 'USDC', token1: 'USDT', initialPrice: 1.0, invertDisplay: false }

Internal V3 price for every pool is now token1/token0 with smaller-USD-value tokens as token0 (stables, or WETH for the BTC pair). Display layer flips for the three pools that need it. All swap math, fee accounting, and tick arithmetic operate on the canonical internal price; the chart, top bar, and tape pass values through displayPrice() before showing them.

Bug 3 — Back-run input was the wrong amount

Symptom

Sandwiches now profitable in principle, but the back-run was leaving small amounts on the table — the searcher's net token position was off by a few percent.

At submission time, the back-run's input amount was set to a simulated expected output of the front-run, computed against pool state before the front actually executed. By the time the back ran, the front had already moved the pool and its actual output differed slightly. The back was trying to swap exactly what was predicted, but the searcher actually held the realised amount.

Real sandwich bundles are atomic — the back's input is whatever the front returned, not a pre-simulated estimate. The fix was a small patch in executeTx:

executeTx — for sandBack txs only
if (tx.type === 'sandBack' && tx.pairedFrontId !== undefined) {
  const frontTx = mempool.find(m => m.id === tx.pairedFrontId);
  if (frontTx && frontTx.executed && frontTx.amountOut > 0) {
    tx.amountIn = frontTx.amountOut; // use realised, not simulated
  }
}

Bug 4 — Naive sizing triggered victim reverts

Symptom

With prices and accounting fixed, sandwiches were profitable on individual bundles but the success rate was poor. Many attempts had the front execute, then the victim revert (slippage exceeded), leaving the searcher's front position stuck with no exit.

The original sandwich sizing aimed to extract maximum impact, ignoring the constraint that the victim's slippage tolerance is finite. If the front pushed price too far, the combined front+victim impact would exceed the victim's slippageBps, the victim's swap would revert, and the searcher would be left with a position that the back-run had to unwind into a worsened pool — almost always at a loss.

The fix — slippage-headroom-aware sizing

Real searchers do this with convex optimisation — solving for the front size that maximises expected profit subject to the victim-revert constraint. The simulator uses a closed-form heuristic that's accurate enough:

Sandwich sizing — current implementation
headroom_bps = victim_slip_bps − victim_impact_bps
if headroom_bps < 5: skip // no profitable attack possible
size_fraction = clamp(0.7 × headroom_bps / victim_impact_bps, 0.2, 2.0)
front_usd = min(victim_size × size_fraction, searcher_capital × 0.7)

// Profit estimate before submission
front_impact_bps = victim_impact_bps × (front_usd / victim_size)
gross_extract = victim_size × front_impact_bps / 10000
fee_cost = 2 × front_usd × pool_fee_bps / 10000
gas_cost = $50
expected_profit = gross_extract − fee_cost − gas_cost

if expected_profit < $50: skip
if front_impact_bps + victim_impact_bps > victim_slip_bps − 2: skip

After this final iteration, the simulator now reports a ~60% sandwich success rate on whale victims in the 5 bp pool — in line with the historical Flashbots data on mainnet sandwich landings. Failed attempts are mostly from gas-tier collisions where another standalone tx slipped between the front and back-run, not from victim reverts.

Bug 5 (briefly) — Systematic upward price bias

Symptom (user-reported)

"When sandwich attacks are present there seems to be a bias towards a rising price in the chart. Is this simply due to the inter-agent dynamics?"

Investigated by running 5 trials × 1,000 blocks each in none mode (no MEV) and measuring price drift:

ModeWETH drift (mean of 5 trials)WBTC driftSandwich attempts
none (before fix)+1.45%+1.54%0
sandwich (before fix)+0.19%+0.96%~10/run
none (after fix)+0.01%+0.01%0
sandwich (after fix)−0.02%−0.01%~10/run

The bias was real but had nothing to do with sandwich attacks. The fund-bias selector was Math.random() < 0.5 ? 'long-eth' : 'long-btc' — every fund was perpetually long-only, never short. Three funds buying continuously, with only symmetric retail flow as counter-pressure, produced the drift.

The fix expanded the bias pool to all four directions (long-eth, short-eth, long-btc, short-btc) distributed across the three funds, plus per-fund bias flipping every 5–15 blocks. Drift collapsed to ±0.01% across both modes — what you'd expect from finite-trade random walk and nothing more.

Sandwich attacks themselves are theoretically price-neutral. The front and back are equal-and-opposite swaps that cancel in expectation; the only price residue the bundle leaves on the pool is whatever the victim's swap would have produced anyway. Any drift caused by sandwich activity is second-order from imperfect bundle atomicity — and in our run with the fix, it sits well below the noise floor.

Lessons from the iteration

What worked

Treating each bug as a hypothesis to be tested with a minimal repro. Every fix was validated by re-running the same 5×1,000-block trial battery. The end-state metrics — sandwich success rate, drift across modes, MEV-extracted-per-day — match published mainnet data within an order of magnitude.

What surprised

The price-convention bug was the most subtle and most consequential. The swap math looked right in isolation. The pool config looked right in isolation. The interaction between them produced a sign-flip that only manifested in specific compound operations — and sandwiching is a compound operation par excellence.

The mainnet evidence is that sandwiching on 30 bp pools barely happens. The simulator reproducing this — without being calibrated to do so — was the validation that the underlying mechanics are right.
