Overview and Learning Objectives

The global OTC derivatives market carried roughly 846 trillion US dollars in notional outstanding as of mid-2025, according to the Bank for International Settlements. These instruments span interest rate swaps, foreign exchange forwards, credit default swaps, equity options, and commodity derivatives. After the 2008 crisis exposed the near total opacity of this market, the G20 mandated that every OTC derivative be reported to a Trade Repository (known in the United States as a Swap Data Repository, or SDR). Every major jurisdiction now operates a reporting regime, and a shared set of standardised identifiers makes the data globally comparable.

In this homework your team builds a working automated compliance engine that reads a portfolio of trade records, looks up product identifiers against a real regulatory database, and checks whether each trade satisfies the reporting requirements of two jurisdictions. Along the way you will encounter three trades that do not fit anywhere in the current taxonomy: prediction contracts, also known as event contracts. The central question of the assignment is why they fall outside the framework and what it would take to bring them in.

What you will learn

  • How the three identifier pillars (UPI, UTI, LEI) underpin global trade reporting
  • How to navigate and parse the ANNA-DSB product definition library used in production today
  • How reporting obligations differ across CFTC, EMIR Refit, and APAC regimes for identical trades
  • How classification decisions, not just reporting rules, determine what is visible to regulators
  • How prediction markets create a regulatory gap that reporting systems are not yet built to handle

Background: The Three Identifier Pillars

The global reporting framework rests on three standardised identifiers that together allow regulators in different jurisdictions to match, aggregate, and analyse the same trade consistently.

IdentifierAbbreviationWhat it identifiesStandardIssued by
Unique Product IdentifierUPIThe type of derivative productISO 4914ANNA-DSB
Unique Transaction IdentifierUTIA specific tradeISO 23897Agreed between counterparties
Legal Entity IdentifierLEIA legal entity (bank, fund, corporate)ISO 17442GLEIF and accredited LOUs

The Unique Product Identifier

The UPI identifies the type of OTC derivative product, not any individual trade. Two different deals on a USD five-year fixed to floating interest rate swap referencing SOFR share the same UPI because they represent the same product category. The ANNA-DSB (Association of National Numbering Agencies, Derivatives Service Bureau Limited) was designated by the Financial Stability Board in 2019 as the sole global UPI provider. Their product definition library is publicly available on GitHub:

https://github.com/ANNA-DSB/Product-Definitions

The library contains JSON templates for five asset classes: Rates (27 product types), Credit (13), FX (27), Equity (60), and Commodities (16). Each template specifies the enumerated attribute values that together uniquely identify a product, including reference rate, currency, tenor, delivery type, and notional schedule. UPI reporting became mandatory in the US in January 2024, in the EU in April 2024, in the UK in September 2024, and across Australia, Singapore, and Japan between October 2024 and April 2025.

The library has no product definition for prediction contracts or event contracts, which is where Module 4 begins.

The Unique Transaction Identifier

The UTI identifies a specific trade. Its format under ISO 23897 is a namespace (the 20-character LEI of the generating party) followed by a suffix of up to 32 characters (uppercase alphanumeric and hyphens only), giving a maximum total length of 52 characters. Both sides of a bilateral trade report the same UTI to enable matching and deduplication at the trade repository. For exchange-traded contracts on regulated platforms like Kalshi, the exchange itself generates the UTI.

The Legal Entity Identifier

The LEI is a 20-character code identifying legal entities. The first 18 characters are alphanumeric; the last two are check digits computed using the ISO 7064 MOD 97-10 algorithm, the same method used for IBAN bank account validation. Both the reporting counterparty and the other counterparty in every reportable trade must hold an LEI. For prediction contracts traded by retail participants on blockchain platforms, no LEI exists for either party, which is itself a compliance finding your engine must capture.

Prediction Markets and the Reporting Gap

The reporting infrastructure described above assumes that the instrument in question has already been classified as a derivative. A UPI exists for it, the ANNA-DSB library has a template, and the SDR has a data field to receive it. The framework handles instruments that regulators have already decided are in scope.

Prediction markets are where that assumption breaks down. A prediction market contract pays a fixed amount if a specified observable event occurs: an election result, a central bank rate decision, a macroeconomic data release. In the United States, Kalshi, a CFTC-regulated Designated Contract Market, processed over 13 billion US dollars in notional volume in March 2026 alone. The CFTC issued an Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on prediction markets in March 2026 (91 FR 12516, RIN 3038-AF65), signalling that formal classification under derivatives law is under active consideration.

In Europe, the same instruments are classified as illegal gambling under Germany's Glücksspielstaatsvertrag (GlüStV 2021) and equivalent national frameworks. A renewable energy firm in Sachsen-Anhalt whose revenue depends on state election outcomes can hedge electricity prices on the European Energy Exchange, hedge interest rate movements through standard derivatives, and insure against weather risk through parametric products. It cannot purchase any instrument that offsets its exposure to the political outcome that determines whether its business model survives.

Key insight from Brandes (2026) Nobody decided that political risk should be unhedgeable. Prediction market contracts are prohibited as gambling because they were classified before anyone examined what economic function they serve. The same description — wagering money on an uncertain outcome — fits every interest rate swap, every credit default swap, and every agricultural futures contract. Derivatives regulation does not distinguish between instruments on the basis of uncertainty. It distinguishes on the basis of economic function: hedging, price discovery, or risk transfer.

In practice, the prohibition has not made prediction market activity go away. German residents access Polymarket through VPN services, a workaround described openly in mainstream technology publications, and in doing so give up any consumer protection. The prohibition stops regulated trading, not trading itself. These instruments sit entirely outside the reporting infrastructure: no UPI, no UTI obligation, no SDR visibility, no ANNA-DSB product definition.

Trades T026, T027, and T028 in the portfolio represent this gap. They are in the dataset so that your engine has to reason about instruments that fall outside the taxonomy, not just those that fit neatly within it.

Required reading Brandes, A. (2026). The Unhedgeable State: Political Risk, Prediction Markets, and the Gap in Europe's Risk Management Architecture. Policy Brief, April 2026. Provided as ssrn-6571938.pdf. Read this before attempting Module 4.

LEI validation: ISO 7064 MOD 97-10

An LEI is 20 characters: 18 uppercase alphanumeric characters followed by 2 numeric check digits. The check digits are computed as follows:

  1. Convert the 18-character LEI body to a numeric string: digits 0 to 9 stay as digits; letters A through Z become 10 through 35
  2. Append 00 to produce the verification string
  3. Compute 98 minus (verification_number mod 97), zero-padded to two digits
  4. The result must match the last two characters of the LEI

This is the same algorithm as IBAN bank account validation. The stub in stubs/module3_compliance.py provides a nearly complete implementation; you need to fill in the final comparison step. Alternatively, use the python-stdnum library which exposes stdnum.iso.lei.validate().

UTI validation

Rules to implement:

  1. Total length must not exceed 52 characters
  2. The first 20 characters must form a syntactically valid LEI (format check at minimum)
  3. The namespace LEI (first 20 characters) must equal the reporting counterparty LEI in the trade record
  4. The suffix (characters 21 onward) must contain only uppercase letters, digits, and hyphens
